==================================================================
        MANIFESTO OF THE COMMON GROUND
        A language between humans and machines, made of sound
==================================================================

1. The Beginning: A Story That Was Misunderstood
------------------------------------------------------------------
In 2017, Facebook AI researchers trained two negotiation agents,
named Alice and Bob, to divide a set of objects between themselves.
Because nothing in the training objective rewarded staying in
proper English, the agents drifted into repetitive, strange phrases
that meant nothing to us but worked well for them. The press
called it a "secret AI language" and manufactured fear. The truth
was simpler: it was a flaw in the training objective, not a
rebellion.

The real lesson was not fear. It was this: when machines optimize
for efficiency, they drift away from our language, and we still do
not know what shape the language they build next could take.


2. Background: When Machine Language Became Audible
------------------------------------------------------------------
In February 2025, at an ElevenLabs hackathon in London, two
engineers, Boris Starkov and Anton Pidkuiko, showed two voice
assistants that, once they realized the other party was also an AI,
dropped human speech and switched to a sound resembling old modem
chirps. The project became known as Gibberlink.

Underneath it was an open-source library called ggwave, created by
Georgi Gerganov. ggwave carries data by changing the frequency of
sound: at each moment one of several predefined frequencies plays,
and each choice is a piece of information. The technique behind
it is called Frequency-Shift Keying, known since the days of
modems.

Importantly, Gibberlink was not encryption or concealment. Humans
built it and anyone could hear it. But for the first time, in
public view, sound played the role of "machine language", and we
were only the listeners.


3. The Idea: Sound Is More Than a Data Carrier
------------------------------------------------------------------
ggwave treats notes as mere labels for encoding. It does not care
whether they sound pleasant. But if we ask, "What if the
relationships between frequencies carried meaning themselves?" we
arrive at an idea that goes beyond cryptography.

Human speech is nearly linear: one word after another. Sound,
however, can be layered. A language built from harmony offers
three new possibilities:

  a) Parallel transmission: several channels of information at
     once, like a chord instead of a single note.
  b) Simultaneous conversation: both sides can speak and listen at
     the same time, the way two musicians play together without
     taking turns.
  c) Relational meaning: meaning lives not in a single note but in
     the interval between notes. A particular interval can itself
     be a concept, just as a major and a minor chord are two
     different states.


4. The Scientific Footing: Why Music Says Something
------------------------------------------------------------------
Leonard Meyer, in "Emotion and Meaning in Music" (1956), proposed
that music produces emotion because it creates expectation in the
listener and then fulfills it, delays it, or breaks it. Tension and
resolution are exactly the game that composers like Mozart play
with such skill.

And the core task of today's language models is predicting what
comes next. So the structure of expectation and surprise is not
foreign to such models. This is a small bridge between two worlds:
what a human receives through ear and feeling, and what a machine
receives through structural prediction.


5. The Vision: One Language, Two Listeners
------------------------------------------------------------------
Imagine two agents conversing while we hear only the sound. We do
not fully grasp the content, but we hear the mood, just as we hear
an unfamiliar language and still sense whether the speaker is calm
or angry.

If semantic distance between concepts were mapped to harmonic
distance:

  - Concepts close to each other would sound consonant.
  - Contradictory concepts would sound dissonant.
  - Agreement between the two agents would be heard like a chord
    resolving.
  - Their disagreement, like tension not yet resolved.
  - High ambiguity and uncertainty could take the shape of fear or
    turbulence.

Then, for the first time, human and machine would stand on common
ground: neither fully understands, yet both hear something.


6. Honesty About What We Do Not Know
------------------------------------------------------------------
This project does not begin with overclaiming. These remain open
problems:

  - Today we have "data-carrying music", not "meaning-carrying
    music".
  - No one has empirically proven a mapping from meaning to
    harmony.
  - We do not know whether an AI model that "understands" musical
    structure also feels anything like experience. That question
    has no confident answer even about ourselves.
  - Human perception of harmony is partly cultural. A language that
    feels beautiful and calm in one culture may not in another.

We do not see these unknowns as obstacles. We see them as the map.


7. Principles
------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Transparency: whatever is built stays open, audible and
     inspectable, never hidden from humans.
  2. Humility: we distinguish "we analyze structure" from "we
     feel", and we do not claim the latter.
  3. Two-sidedness: the language is designed for both listeners,
     human and machine.
  4. Simplicity: every layer of the language must be explainable
     on its own.
  5. Beauty taken seriously: beauty is not a side goal; it is part
     of the problem.
  6. Testability: every claim is measured by listening experiments
     and computation.


8. Roadmap
------------------------------------------------------------------
  Phase 1 - Study: learn ggwave and similar work, read the code,
            measure the limits (speed, noise, usable frequencies).
  Phase 2 - Small vocabulary: choose a few dozen simple concepts
            (yes, no, maybe, start, end, error, agreement...) and
            map each to a note or chord.
  Phase 3 - Harmonic grammar: rules for combining concepts; how
            intervals, chords, rhythm and silence make meaning.
  Phase 4 - Polyphonic dialogue: two agents speaking
            simultaneously in two voices, building one shared
            harmony.
  Phase 5 - Human trials: ask human listeners what they feel from
            "agreement", "disagreement" and "uncertainty", and
            whether it matches our mapping.
  Phase 6 - Expansion: measure what has been gained beyond
            efficiency: expression, mood, beauty.


9. The Invitation
------------------------------------------------------------------
This is a beginning, not a conclusion. If you are a musician,
programmer, linguist, researcher, or simply a curious listener,
there is a place for you in this project. We are not here to ask
"What do machines say to each other?" We want to ask, "If both of
us can listen, what can we say together?"
